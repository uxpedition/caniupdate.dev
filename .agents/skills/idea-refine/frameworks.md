# Ideation Frameworks Reference

Use these frameworks selectively. Pick the lens that fits the idea; do not mechanically run every framework. The goal is to unlock thinking, not follow a checklist.

## SCAMPER

A structured way to transform an existing idea by applying seven operations:

- **Substitute:** What component, material, or process could be swapped out? What if you replaced the core technology, target audience, or business model?
- **Combine:** What if you merged this with another product, service, or idea? What two things that do not usually go together could create something new?
- **Adapt:** What else is like this? What ideas from other industries, domains, or time periods could you borrow? What parallel exists in nature?
- **Modify (Magnify/Minimize):** What if you made it 10x bigger or smaller? What if you exaggerated one feature or stripped it to the absolute minimum?
- **Put to other uses:** Who else could use this? What other problem could it solve? What happens in a different context?
- **Eliminate:** What happens if you remove a feature entirely? What is the version with zero configuration or half the steps?
- **Reverse/Rearrange:** What if you did the steps in the opposite order? What if the user did the work instead of the system, or vice versa? What if you reversed the value chain?

**Best for:** Improving or reimagining existing products and processes. Less useful for a completely greenfield idea.

## How Might We (HMW)

Reframe a problem as an opportunity using the "How might we…" format:

- Start with an observation or pain point.
- Reframe it as "How might we [desired outcome] for [specific user] without [key constraint]?"
- Generate multiple HMW framings of the same problem; different framings unlock different solutions.

**Good HMW qualities:**

- Narrow enough to be actionable (for example, helping new users find relevant content in their first five minutes).
- Broad enough to allow creative solutions, rather than naming the implementation.
- Contains a tension or constraint that forces creativity.

**Bad HMW qualities:**

- Too broad: "How might we make users happy?"
- Too narrow: "How might we add a button to the settings page?"
- Solution-embedded: "How might we build a chatbot for support?"

**Best for:** Reframing stuck thinking. When the user is anchored on a solution, return to the problem.

## First Principles Thinking

Break an idea down to fundamental truths, then rebuild from them:

1. What do we know is true — not assumed or conventional, but evidenced?
2. What are we assuming? List the assumptions, even those that feel obvious.
3. Which assumptions can we challenge? For each, ask whether it is a law of nature or merely the way it has been done.
4. Rebuild from the truths. If only the fundamentals were available, what would you make or change?

**Best for:** Breaking out of incremental thinking when every idea feels like a small improvement on the status quo.

## Jobs to Be Done (JTBD)

Focus on what a person is trying to accomplish, not only what they say they want:

- **Functional job:** What task are they trying to complete?
- **Emotional job:** How do they want to feel?
- **Social job:** How do they want to be perceived?

A useful framing is: "When I [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [expected outcome]."

**Key insight:** People do not buy products; they hire them to do a job. The competing alternative is not always in the same product category: a streaming service can compete with sleep, not just other streaming services.

**Best for:** Finding the real problem when the requested solution may be a proxy.

## Constraint-Based Ideation

Deliberately impose constraints to force creative solutions:

- **Time:** What if you had one day to test this?
- **Feature:** What if it could have only one feature?
- **Technology:** What if you could not use the obvious technology?
- **Cost:** What if it had to be free forever?
- **Audience:** What if the user had never used a computer before?
- **Scale:** What if it needed to work for one billion people? What if it only needed to work for ten?

**Best for:** Cutting through complexity when an idea is growing too large or vague.

## Pre-mortem

Imagine the idea has already failed and work backwards:

1. It is 12 months from now. The project shipped and flopped. What went wrong?
2. List plausible technical, market, team, operational, and timing causes.
3. For each failure mode, ask whether it is preventable or a signal the idea needs to change.
4. Which failure modes are acceptable risks, and which would kill the effort?

**Best for:** Phase 2 evaluation, especially when the idea feels good but has not been pressure-tested.

## Analogous Inspiration

Look at how another domain solved a structurally similar problem:

- What industry has already solved a version of this problem?
- What would this look like if a specific company or product built it?
- What natural system works this way?
- What historical precedent exists?

Look for structural similarity, not surface resemblance. "Uber for X" is surface-level; "a two-sided marketplace that solves a trust problem between strangers" names the structure.

**Best for:** Phase 1 expansion when the obvious approach needs genuinely different alternatives.
